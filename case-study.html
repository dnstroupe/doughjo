DOUGHJO · AN INDEPENDENT UX PROJECT

MAKE THE BOX
PART OF THE FUN.

I wanted to design something with more personality without making it harder to use. Doughjo turns a box of donuts into a small collection of favorites, with anime inspired names and a mascot that belongs to the shop.

THE IDEA

Fourteen flavors, one shop, and a box of six or twelve. Customers can choose by character, taste, or curiosity. The playful name gets their attention; the description and price help them decide.

Purvé Hawaii was a light reference for the idea of expressive donut names paired with straightforward flavors. Doughjo uses its own name, mascot, visual direction, menu concepts, and ordering interaction.

THE DESIGN QUESTION

Can building an assortment feel playful while the customer still understands what is in the box, what it costs, and how to change it?

That is the question this concept explores. It is not a finding from completed user research.

WHAT I BUILT

The work covers brand direction, the 14 flavor roster, a mobile layout, a visual box builder, recovery states, guest checkout, an editable Figma flow, and a browser prototype. It uses fictional restaurant information and stops at a simulated order confirmation.

HOW THE JOURNEY WORKS

  1. Explore the roster. Search matches donut names, character names, and descriptions. Flavor categories narrow the choices without pretending to be dietary filters.
  2. Build a squad. Each addition appears in a numbered box slot. The count, progress, item list, and price update together. Repeated favorites are allowed.
  3. Make changes. Selecting a slot opens explicit move, swap, and remove controls. Swapping keeps the original until a replacement is selected. Removing an item offers Undo.
  4. Review the collection. A short reveal celebrates a completed box. A text list makes every flavor available without depending on the illustrations.
  5. Check out as a guest. The final action repeats the total. No account is required, and the demo never collects payment information.

WHY THESE CHOICES

PLAYFUL OUTSIDE. CLEAR INSIDE.

Butter yellow, purple, chunky type, ink outlines, and a tilted illustration bring energy to the page. The prices, ingredient descriptions, and controls use simpler type and consistent placement.

THE BOX IS A WORKSPACE

On a wide screen, the box stays beside the roster. On a phone, a fixed summary links to it. Each slot has an accessible name that includes its position and flavor. Moving a donut uses buttons instead of requiring a drag gesture.

PERSONALITY SHOULD NOT TAKE OVER

Doughshi introduces the shop, helps narrow the flavor roster, reacts as the box fills, and celebrates the finished squad. Selection and reveal animations are brief. A motion control and the system preference can turn animation off. Checkout focuses on the order rather than adding more character animation.

NO SURPRISE SUBSTITUTIONS

A sold out flavor stays visible in the box and blocks checkout until the customer changes it. A different donut may have a different price or ingredients, so the app does not make that choice for them.

BRINGING THE MENU UP TO THE PROMISE

The first version had a rich hero illustration but simpler donuts in the menu. That gap made choosing feel less exciting than arriving. I replaced the menu art with a consistent set of 14 detailed donut images and carried those same images into the box and flavor details.

Doughshi now has a useful role throughout the visit. The flavor desk narrows choices, a suggested squad gives customers a starting point they can review and undo, and pairing suggestions connect donuts to three optional drinks. Drinks have separate quantities and never use a donut slot.

The box opens with a small reveal, but every flavor and the full price remain available as text. Motion is optional. The added personality supports the order instead of interrupting it.

THE HARDER STATES

  • A full box cannot accept an extra donut.
  • Switching from twelve to six asks before removing items and offers Undo.
  • Invalid saved browser data is filtered before it is restored.
  • Empty search results offer a way back to the full roster.
  • A failed demo order keeps the box and explains that no order was created.
  • Repeated clicks after confirmation cannot create another order from the same form because the form is replaced.

WHAT THIS ADDS TO MY PORTFOLIO

Gather focused on a calm restaurant ordering journey. Doughjo explores a stronger brand personality, a configurable assortment, reversible choices, and more expressive visual feedback. The growth is in the range of design decisions and interaction states. I am not claiming a measured improvement in usability.

CHECKS AND NEXT STEPS

Twenty four automated checks cover roster data, totals, capacity, reordering, unavailable items, and recovery of saved selections. Browser review covers the main ordering journey and selected recovery states. The project kit records exactly what was checked.

The next research step is to watch people build and change a box, explain its total, and recover from a sold out item. No participant sessions, conversion results, or business outcomes are claimed.
